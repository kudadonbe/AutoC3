## Chapter 01 — Engine Components and Workshop Safety

**Q: What does an internal combustion (IC) engine do?**
It converts the chemical energy of fuel into mechanical energy. Combustion
takes place inside the cylinder. Two main types: petrol (spark ignition) and
diesel (compression ignition).

**Q: Describe the basic working principle.**
Combustion of the fuel-air mixture produces expanding gases. The gases push the
piston (heat energy → mechanical energy). Piston motion transfers to the
crankshaft via the connecting rod, producing rotary motion.

**Q: Name the four strokes of the four-stroke cycle (Otto, 1876).**
1. **Intake (suction):** piston down, inlet valve open, exhaust closed; air-fuel
   (petrol) or air (diesel) enters.
2. **Compression:** both valves closed, piston up; pressure and temperature
   rise. At the end: petrol = spark plug ignites; diesel = fuel injected, ignites
   from compression heat.
3. **Power (expansion):** combustion pushes piston down — the only power-producing
   stroke; crankshaft converts it to rotation.
4. **Exhaust:** exhaust valve open, inlet closed, piston up, pushes out burnt
   gases.

**Q: How does a two-stroke engine differ?**
All four operations occur in two strokes (one crankshaft revolution). Simple,
light, more power for its size, but less fuel-efficient and more polluting. Used
in motorbikes, scooters, chainsaws.

**Q: Petrol vs diesel — key differences.**
- Air-fuel mixing: petrol before intake (carburetor/injector); diesel inside
  cylinder.
- Ignition: petrol = spark plug; diesel = compression heat.
- Compression ratio: petrol 6:1–10:1; diesel 14:1–22:1.
- Efficiency: diesel higher.

**Q: Function of key components.**
Piston = converts gas pressure to motion; valves = control intake/exhaust;
spark plug/injector = initiates combustion; crankshaft = reciprocating to
rotary; flywheel = smooths rotation.

**Q: List main workshop safety practices.**
Wear PPE (goggles, gloves, hearing protection, closed-toe shoes, no loose
clothing/jewellery); keep area clean, lit and ventilated; secure vehicle on jack
stands; store flammables away from heat; disconnect battery before electrical
work; never work on a hot engine; know fire-extinguisher locations; report
accidents.

## Chapter 02 — Move, Position and Park Vehicle

**Q: How do you move a manual-transmission vehicle from rest?**
Start in neutral with handbrake on; press clutch fully and select first gear;
raise revs to ~1,500; slowly lift clutch to the biting point; release handbrake
and ease the clutch out while adding gas.

**Q: How do you move an automatic vehicle?**
Press the brake pedal and start the engine; shift to Drive; release parking
brake and foot brake; gently press the gas.

**Q: General parking steps.**
Find a legal spot → signal → position the vehicle → check mirrors and blind
spots → manoeuvre slowly → straighten out → secure (parking brake, Park or
neutral, engine off, remove key).

**Q: Types of parking covered.**
Perpendicular (head-in or back-in — back-in is safer when exiting), angle
parking, and parallel parking (find a space ~1.5× car length).

**Q: Step-by-step safe lifting with a floor jack and jack stands.**
Park level, engage parking brake, chock wheels → position jack at the jacking
point → raise slowly → insert jack stands at frame/axle support points → lower
the vehicle onto the stands → do a stability check → work → to finish, raise
slightly, remove stands, lower fully.

**Q: What is the WOFT daily check?**
**W**ater, **O**il, **F**an belt, **T**ires — basic daily maintenance.

**Q: Safe positioning rules.**
Park on level ground (max ~2% grade), position for forward-only first movement,
Park (auto) or low gear (manual), engage parking brake and use wheel chocks.

## Chapter 03 — Wash and Clean Vehicle Body and Interior

**Q: What is the correct washing order?**
Interior first, then exterior, always working top to bottom. Wheels are cleaned
first (dirtiest) with a separate brush and bucket.

**Q: What is the two-bucket method and why use it?**
One bucket holds soapy water, the second holds clean water to rinse the wash
mitt. It reduces scratches/swirl marks by removing trapped grit before
re-loading soap.

**Q: Key washing rules.**
Work in shade so soap doesn't dry; ensure the body is cool; pre-rinse to remove
loose dirt; wash top-down in straight lines (not circles); use pH-neutral car
soap (never dish soap) and a microfiber mitt.

**Q: How should you dry the car?**
Do not air dry (causes water spots). Pat dry top-down with clean microfiber
towels or a chamois.

**Q: Extra shine / protection steps.**
Use dedicated glass cleaner for streak-free windows; apply wax or sealant to
protect paint; apply tire shine and trim dressing.

## Chapter 04 — Inspection and Servicing Engine

**Q: Difference between engine inspection and engine service.**
Inspection = proactively checking components for wear, damage or malfunction.
Service = the practical maintenance/repair actions (preventive or corrective)
taken to fix findings and keep the engine running well.

**Q: Three main aspects of an engine inspection.**
1. **Visual checks** — cracks in belts/hoses, corrosion, fluid leaks, fasteners.
2. **Fluid level and quality checks** — oil, coolant, brake fluid, contamination.
3. **Diagnostic testing** — scan OBD trouble codes.

**Q: What do exhaust smoke colours indicate?**
- **Blue:** burning oil (worn valve seals or piston rings).
- **White:** head gasket leak (coolant entering combustion chamber).
- **Black:** rich fuel mixture.

**Q: What does milky residue on the oil cap mean?**
Coolant has mixed with the oil — possibly a blown head gasket.

**Q: Steps to perform a DIY OBD diagnostic test.**
Locate the OBD port (under the dash) → connect the scanner's 16-pin plug with
the car off → turn ignition ON (not the engine) → read the trouble codes.

**Q: Common engine service tasks.**
Oil and filter change; air/fuel filter replacement; replacing worn parts (spark
plugs, belts, hoses); fluid top-ups and flushes; cleaning and adjustments (idle
speed, ignition timing).

## Chapter 06 — Petrol Fuel System

**Q: List petrol fuel system components and their functions.**
- **Fuel tank** — stores petrol (filler neck, fuel cap, baffles, vent pipes).
- **Fuel pump** — draws fuel and delivers it under pressure.
- **Fuel filter** — removes dirt/contaminants.
- **Fuel lines** — carry fuel from tank to engine.
- **Carburetor** (older) — mechanically mixes air and fuel.
- **Fuel injectors** (modern) — electronically spray atomized fuel.
- **Fuel pressure regulator** — maintains correct injector pressure.
- **Fuel rail** — distributes fuel to the injectors.

**Q: Types of fuel pumps.**
Mechanical (older, cam/crank driven, ~3–5 PSI); electric (modern, in-tank,
50–90 PSI); high-pressure (GDI/direct injection, fed by a low-pressure in-tank
pump).

**Q: Symptoms of a clogged fuel filter.**
Hard starting, stalling, misfires/rough idle, loss of power, poor fuel economy,
whining fuel pump, check-engine light (running lean).

**Q: Types of petrol fuel delivery (injection) systems.**
Single-point (throttle body); multi-point (port, one injector per cylinder);
sequential (timed to each intake stroke); direct injection (GDI, high-pressure
into the chamber — most precise).

**Q: Name the main carburetor components.**
Float chamber (bowl), venturi, jets, choke valve (cold start, rich mixture),
throttle valve/plate (controls mixture entering the engine).

## Chapter 07 — Diesel Fuel System

**Q: How does a diesel engine ignite fuel?**
By compression — air is compressed to a high temperature, then diesel is
injected and spontaneously combusts (no spark plug).

**Q: List the main diesel fuel system components in order.**
Fuel tank → lift/transfer pump (low pressure) → fuel filter / water separator →
high-pressure pump → common rail / injector system → injector nozzles →
combustion chamber.

**Q: Function of the diesel lift pump.**
A low-pressure pump that transfers fuel from the tank to the high-pressure
injection pump, ensuring clean, consistent supply and preventing fuel
starvation.

**Q: Why is the diesel fuel filter / water separator important?**
It removes contaminants, dirt and **water** — critical for protecting expensive
high-pressure common-rail injectors and pumps.

**Q: What is a high-pressure fuel pump (HPFP) and how is it driven?**
It pressurizes fuel for direct injection; usually mechanically driven by a lobe
on the engine camshaft. High pressure gives a fine atomized spray for better
combustion, economy and lower emissions.

**Q: Main types of high-pressure diesel systems.**
High-Pressure Common Rail (HPCR — pump feeds a shared rail/accumulator);
Electronic Unit Injector (EUI); Hydraulically Actuated EUI (HEUI); distributor
pumps and inline (individual) pumps in older engines.

**Q: Key parts of a diesel injector.**
Nozzle/spray tip, needle valve/pintle, solenoid/piezo actuator, pressure spring,
injector body, fuel inlet with filter basket.

**Q: What is the ECU/ECM and its three operating stages?**
The engine's "brain." It works in a loop: **input** (sensors — crank/cam
position, MAF/MAP, O2, coolant temp, throttle) → **processing** (microcomputer
compares to maps) → **output** (actuators — injectors, coils, throttle, VVT).

## Chapter 08 — Ignition System

**Q: Purpose of the ignition system.**
To generate a high-voltage spark that ignites the compressed air-fuel mixture in
the cylinders.

**Q: How does the ignition system work (two circuits)?**
- **Primary (low-voltage):** 12 V from the battery flows through the ignition
  switch into the coil's primary winding, creating a magnetic field.
- **Secondary (high-voltage):** a trigger interrupts the primary current; the
  collapsing field induces a very high voltage (40,000–100,000 V) in the
  secondary winding, sent to the correct spark plug for combustion.

**Q: Main ignition components.**
Battery, ignition switch, ignition coil (transformer), spark plugs, distributor
(older — routes spark in firing order, uses points and condenser), and ECU /
ignition module (modern — electronic timing, no distributor).

**Q: Types of spark plugs.**
Copper (high conductivity, wears fast); platinum (longer life); double platinum
(for waste-spark systems); iridium (most durable, fine wire, most efficient
spark).

**Q: Four types of ignition systems (evolution).**
1. Conventional breaker-point (mechanical points + condenser).
2. Electronic ignition (solid-state, still uses a distributor).
3. Distributorless (DIS) — coils per pair of cylinders, computer-timed.
4. Coil-on-plug (COP) — individual coil on each plug; most advanced.

**Q: Advantages of coil-on-plug (COP).**
Stronger/precise sparks (better combustion and power), greater efficiency and
lower emissions, higher reliability (no plug wires; replace only one failed
coil), longer service life with iridium/platinum plugs.

## Chapter 09 — Steering System

**Q: Purpose of the steering system.**
Converts the driver's rotational input at the steering wheel into the swiveling
motion of the wheels to change direction. Provides directional control, force
conversion, road feedback/stability and safety.

**Q: Main steering components.**
Steering wheel; steering column (houses shaft, universal joints, collapsible
safety feature, turn-signal switch); steering gear (rack-and-pinion or
recirculating ball); linkage (tie rods, ball joints, steering arms, knuckles).

**Q: Compare rack-and-pinion and recirculating ball.**
Rack-and-pinion: most common in modern cars, direct and precise — a pinion gear
moves a toothed rack left/right. Recirculating ball: older, used in trucks/large
vehicles — a worm gear turns a sector gear with ball bearings to cut friction.

**Q: Types of power steering.**
- **Hydraulic (HPS):** engine-driven pump pressurizes fluid; a rotary valve
  directs it to a cylinder. Responsive but constantly loads the engine (worse
  economy) and needs fluid maintenance.
- **Electric (EPS):** an electric motor assists only when steering; a torque
  sensor + ECU vary assist by speed. More efficient, no fluid leaks.
- Others: steer-by-wire, four-wheel steering, manual steering.

**Q: Key EPS components.**
Electric motor, ECU, torque sensor, steering angle sensor, reduction gear.

## Chapter 10 — Transmission System (Overview + Manual)

**Q: Purpose of the transmission.**
Transfers engine power to the wheels, managing speed and torque via gear ratios
so the engine stays in its optimal RPM range — high torque for starting/climbing,
low torque for high-speed cruising.

**Q: Relationship between torque and speed.**
Inverse, for constant power (P = T × ω). A higher gear ratio increases torque
but reduces speed; an overdrive (ratio < 1:1) increases speed but reduces torque.

**Q: Common transmission types.**
Manual (MT), Automatic (AT), CVT (continuously variable), DCT (dual-clutch),
Sequential, and AMT (automated manual).

**Q: Basic parts of a manual transmission.**
Clutch (connects/disconnects engine and gearbox), gearbox (housing), shafts
(input, output, countershaft/layshaft), gears, and shift mechanism (lever, shift
forks, synchronizers).

**Q: Three gearbox types.**
Sliding mesh (earliest — gears slide on a splined shaft); constant mesh (gears
always engaged); synchromesh (synchronizers match speeds for smooth, silent
shifts).

**Q: How is a gear ratio calculated?**
Ratio = driven teeth ÷ driving teeth. >1:1 = reduction/underdrive (more torque,
less speed); <1:1 = overdrive (more speed, less torque). Overall ratio =
transmission ratio × final-drive ratio.

**Q: Briefly, how do CVT, DCT, sequential and AMT work?**
- **CVT:** two variable-diameter pulleys and a belt give infinite ratios; keeps
  engine at its most efficient RPM.
- **DCT:** two clutches (one for odd gears, one for even/reverse) pre-select the
  next gear for near-instant shifts; no clutch pedal.
- **Sequential:** linear up/down selection (1-2-3…), uses dog clutches; common in
  motorcycles and race cars.
- **AMT:** a manual gearbox with computer-controlled clutch and shifting; no
  clutch pedal, cheaper and efficient but can feel jerky.

## Chapter 11 — Automatic Transmission

**Q: What is an automatic transmission?**
A gearbox that changes gear ratios automatically based on speed, load and
throttle, with no clutch pedal or manual shifting.

**Q: Four main parts / how it works.**
1. **Torque converter** — fluid coupling replacing the clutch.
2. **Planetary gear sets** — provide the gear ratios.
3. **Hydraulic system and valve body** — control gear shifts with ATF.
4. **ECU / TCU** — electronic control.

**Q: Parts of a torque converter.**
Impeller (pump, engine-driven, flings fluid out); turbine (driven by the fluid,
turns the input shaft); stator (redirects returning fluid, multiplies torque).
Modern units add a lockup clutch for direct drive at speed.

**Q: Name the planetary gear set parts.**
Sun gear (centre), planet gears (orbit on a carrier), ring gear (outer internal
teeth), and the carrier. Holding one member while driving another gives
reduction, overdrive or reverse.

**Q: Role of the valve body.**
The hydraulic "brain" — manual valve, shift valves, solenoids and pressure
regulators direct pressurized ATF to clutches and bands to change gears.

**Q: Difference between an ECU and a TCU.**
ECU = generic electronic control unit (e.g. Engine Control Unit). TCU = a
specific unit dedicated to managing the automatic transmission (shift timing and
quality from sensor inputs).

**Q: Four jobs of automatic transmission fluid (ATF).**
1. Transmits engine power (hydrodynamics, in the torque converter).
2. Operates gear shifting (hydraulics, via the valve body).
3. Provides managed friction for clutch packs (friction modifiers).
4. Cools, lubricates and cleans the system.

## Chapter 12 — Brake System

**Q: What does a brake system do?**
Uses friction to slow/stop the vehicle by converting kinetic energy into thermal
energy (heat), using hydraulic pressure.

**Q: Four main types of brakes.**
Disc brakes (caliper squeezes pads onto a rotor — strong, good cooling, usually
front); drum brakes (shoes push out against a drum — cheaper, often rear); ABS
(prevents wheel lock); regenerative braking (EV/hybrid — recovers energy to the
battery).

**Q: Key brake system components.**
Brake pedal, master cylinder (creates hydraulic pressure; tandem for safety),
brake fluid and lines, brake booster/servo (amplifies pedal force), calipers,
pads, rotors/discs, shoes and drums, parking brake.

**Q: How does the hydraulic brake system work?**
Pedal pushes a pushrod and piston in the master cylinder → fluid is pressurized →
pressure travels through lines to calipers/wheel cylinders → pads/shoes press the
rotor/drum → friction slows the wheel → return springs release when the pedal is
let go.

**Q: How does ABS work?**
Wheel-speed sensors feed the ECU; if a wheel is about to lock, the ECU signals
the modulator to reduce pressure to that wheel, then reapply once traction
returns — cycling up to 15–20 times per second. Helps keep steering control.

**Q: ABS configurations.**
Four-channel/four-sensor (best, modern); three-channel (front independent, rear
together); one-channel (rear only, older trucks/vans).

**Q: What is the handbrake/parking brake?**
A secondary brake independent of the hydraulic foot brakes (also called
emergency/e-brake). Holds the vehicle stationary (especially on inclines) and is
a backup. Mechanical types use lever → cables → equalizer yoke → rear brakes with
a ratchet/pawl lock; EPB uses a button and electric actuators on the rear
calipers.

**Q: Common brake faults and likely causes.**
Squealing/grinding = worn pads; soft/spongy pedal = air or low fluid; hard pedal
= booster/vacuum fault or seized caliper; vibration = warped rotors; pulling to
one side = uneven wear or stuck caliper; burning smell = overheated/dragging
brakes.

## Chapter 13 — Auto Electric System

**Q: What are the three core parts of the car electrical system?**
**Battery** (stores energy, supplies starting current — 12 V DC), **alternator**
(converts mechanical to electrical energy once running, recharges the battery),
and **starter motor** (cranks the engine). Plus wiring, fuses/relays and ECUs.

**Q: Purpose and key parts of the starting system.**
Converts stored chemical energy into mechanical cranking power (must spin the
engine ~200–300 RPM). Parts: battery, ignition switch, starter solenoid, starter
motor, and flywheel ring gear.

**Q: Step-by-step starting operation.**
Driver turns key/presses start → low current to the solenoid → solenoid pushes
the pinion into the flywheel ring gear → solenoid closes heavy contacts, sending
high current to the motor → starter cranks the engine until it fires → key
released, return spring disengages the pinion.

**Q: What do the solenoid's two windings do?**
Pull-in winding engages the drive gear; hold-in winding keeps the plunger
engaged with less current.

**Q: Common starting-system faults.**
Repeated clicking = dead battery/corroded terminals; single loud click = bad
solenoid or jammed starter; whirring with no crank = starter spins but doesn't
engage the flywheel; no sound = blown fuse, bad ignition switch, or neutral
safety switch.

**Q: What is OBD, MIL and DLC?**
OBD = On-Board Diagnostics (self-diagnosis). MIL = Malfunction Indicator Lamp
("check engine" light). DLC = Data Link Connector — the 16-pin trapezoidal port,
within ~3 ft of the steering wheel; standard on US cars since 1996.

**Q: How do you read a DTC like P0300?**
5 characters: 1st letter = system (P = powertrain, B = body, C = chassis, U =
network); 2nd digit = 0 generic / 1 manufacturer-specific; 3rd digit = sub-system
(3 = misfire, 7 = transmission); last two = specific fault. P0300 = generic
engine cylinder misfire.

## Chapter 14 — Final Drive Assembly / Drive Types

**Q: What is the final drive assembly?**
The last set of gears in the drivetrain that reduces engine RPM and increases
torque before power reaches the wheels. It contains the differential and sits in
the transaxle (FWD) or rear axle (RWD).

**Q: Main drivetrain components.**
Transmission (adjusts speed/torque), driveshaft/propeller shaft (transfers torque
to the differential), differential (lets axle wheels turn at different speeds),
axles/half-shafts (connect differential to wheels), transfer case (4WD/AWD —
splits power, provides low range).

**Q: What does the differential do and why is it needed?**
Transmits power to the wheels while allowing them to rotate at different speeds —
essential when turning corners. Types: open, limited-slip, locking,
torque-vectoring.

**Q: What is a half-shaft?**
An axle shaft from the differential/transaxle to one wheel; two per drive axle.
In independent suspension they use CV joints at both ends to flex with steering
and suspension movement.

**Q: Compare the four drive types.**
- **FWD:** power to front wheels (steer + drive). Compact, lighter, better
  economy, good traction in poor weather, more interior space, lower cost.
- **RWD:** power to rear wheels; front only steers. Balanced (~50:50) weight,
  better handling and acceleration, no torque steer, good for towing/performance;
  weaker in snow/ice.
- **4WD (4x4):** power to all wheels, usually driver-selected, uses a transfer
  case; modes 2WD / 4H (slippery) / 4L (extreme off-road, max torque). For
  off-road use.
- **AWD:** automatically sends power to all wheels as needed; uses a centre
  differential/viscous coupling. For on-road traction in rain/snow, not severe
  off-roading.

## Chapter 15 — Auto Air-Condition (A/C) System

**Q: What principle does the A/C system work on?**
Thermodynamics — it moves heat from inside the cabin to the outside using a
refrigerant that cycles between liquid and gas. Functions: cooling,
dehumidifying and circulating cabin air.

**Q: List the main A/C components and the refrigeration cycle.**
1. **Compressor** — the "heart"; compresses low-pressure gas into high-pressure,
   high-temperature gas.
2. **Condenser** — dissipates heat; refrigerant condenses to high-pressure liquid.
3. **Receiver/dryer or accumulator** — stores liquid, removes moisture/debris.
4. **Expansion valve or orifice tube** — pressure drops, temperature falls to a
   cool liquid/gas mix.
5. **Evaporator** — refrigerant absorbs cabin heat and vaporizes, cooling and
   dehumidifying the air.
6. **Blower fan** — circulates the cooled air; gas returns to the compressor.

**Q: What is the compressor clutch and its failure symptoms?**
It engages/disengages the compressor from the drive belt (pulley, coil, pressure
plate). Failure: squealing/grinding noise, AC blowing hot, burning smell, or a
seized clutch burning/snapping the belt.

**Q: Common A/C faults.**
Warm/no cold air (low refrigerant from leaks, faulty compressor, blocked
condenser); weak airflow (clogged cabin filter, failing blower motor, blocked
vents); unusual noises/smells (musty = mold in evaporator; hissing = leak;
grinding = compressor); electrical issues (blown fuses/relays, faulty sensors).

**Q: Methods to detect a refrigerant leak.**
Visual inspection (oily residue, hissing); UV dye detection; electronic leak
detector ("sniffer"); soapy-water bubble test; nitrogen pressure test (when the
system is empty).

## Chapter 16 — Suspension System

**Q: Key functions of the suspension system.**
Ride comfort (absorbs/dampens road impacts), tire contact (keeps tires on the
road), handling and control (manages body movement for steering/cornering), and
vehicle support (supports weight, maintains ride height).

**Q: Main suspension components.**
Springs, shock absorbers (dampers), struts, control arms (wishbones), sway bars
(anti-roll bars), and ball joints and bushings.

**Q: Types of springs.**
Coil; leaf (stacked steel strips — trucks/heavy vehicles, high load); torsion bar
(twisting steel rod, adjustable height — SUVs/off-road); air spring (air-filled
bladders, self-leveling — luxury vehicles, buses, trucks).

**Q: Difference between a shock absorber and a strut.**
A shock absorber converts spring movement (kinetic energy) into heat to stop
bouncing. A strut combines a coil spring and a shock absorber into one structural
unit (and can serve as a steering pivot).

**Q: MacPherson strut vs double wishbone.**
MacPherson strut = single lower control arm plus a combined spring/shock that
also acts as the steering pivot (simple, common). Double wishbone = two A-arms
for precise geometry control and superior handling, comfort, tunability,
durability and reduced tire wear.

**Q: Categories of suspension systems.**
Dependent (wheels on an axle linked — e.g. leaf spring; strong, simple, good for
loads); semi-independent (a compromise with some flexibility); independent (each
wheel moves separately — MacPherson strut, double wishbone, multi-link, air
suspension; better comfort, handling and tire contact).

**Q: What is multi-link suspension?**
An independent system using three or more control links per wheel, giving
engineers flexibility to tune comfort, handling, alignment and stability.
